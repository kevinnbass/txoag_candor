# Mistakes in the State's Court Filings: Bass v. Texas Tech

Kevin Bass was a medical student at Texas Tech University Health Sciences Center (TTUHSC) until the school dismissed him in January 2024. He sued school officials in federal court and in Texas state court. The Texas Attorney General's office is their lawyer in both cases. Assistant Attorney General Scott D. Smith signed every filing reviewed here.

When lawyers file papers in court, judges rely on them to describe the record accurately: what the other side's complaint says, what an exhibit says, what an earlier court decision held. This page lists places where the State's filings did not do that. Each entry gives the exact words the filing used, what the record actually shows, and a link to the page so you can check it yourself.

## Said a court decision says something it does not

Lawyers cite earlier court decisions to show what the law is. Readers, judges included, often trust the description instead of reading the case. In these entries, the description does not match the decision.

### 1. Cited three Texas Supreme Court cases for a rule none of them states

**In:** The State's response opposing Mr. Bass's motion for a new trial, filed Apr. 14, 2026, pages 3–4.

**What the filing says:**

> There, the Court held that a party who has knowledge of facts that would prompt a reasonable inquiry cannot rely on Rule 306a to extend deadlines.

> Texas courts have consistently rejected Rule 306a relief in similar circumstances where the movant had information that should have prompted further inquiry. See, e.g., In re Lynd Co., … John v. Marshall Health Servs., Inc., …

> The Texas Supreme Court has emphasized that litigants have a duty to monitor their cases and to take reasonable steps to protect their interests. See In re Lynd Co., 195 S.W.3d at 685.

**What the record shows:** Mr. Bass asked for more time under Rule 306a, a court rule for people who learn of a ruling late. The State answered that a person who had reason to ask questions cannot use that rule, and cited three Texas Supreme Court decisions. None of them says that. *Gillis* (1987) holds that the person must prove, in the trial court, when they learned of the ruling. *Lynd* (2006) never mentions a duty to monitor a case, and the party claiming late notice won. *John* (2001) also ruled for the party claiming late notice. None of the three discusses what someone must do after being warned of a problem.

**Why it matters:** A central argument in the response was that Mr. Bass lost the protection of the rule because he had been warned that court notices were not reaching him. The three cases were offered as the legal basis for that argument, and they do not provide it.

### 2. Cited a Supreme Court case for the opposite of what it says

**In:** The State's response opposing the motion to disqualify its lawyer (Doc. 50), filed Apr. 28, 2026, pages 4–5 and 12.

**What the filing says:**

> The Fifth Circuit has squarely rejected disqualification based on the “mere possibility of conflict,” requiring instead a real and present conflict that threatens the fairness or efficiency of the proceeding. Dresser, 972 F.2d at 543; see also Wheat v. United States, 486 U.S. 153, 164 (1988).

> The Fifth Circuit has rejected disqualification based on “speculative or potential conflicts,” emphasizing that courts must avoid disrupting representation based on contingencies that may never arise. Dresser, 972 F.2d at 543; Wheat, 486 U.S. at 164.

**What the record shows:** Mr. Bass had asked the court to disqualify the State's lawyer from also representing a second defendant, Darren Gibson, arguing that the joint representation created a conflict of interest. The response told the court that disqualification requires "a real and present conflict" and that the Fifth Circuit "has rejected disqualification based on 'speculative or potential conflicts.'" It cited two cases for that. In *Wheat v. United States* (1988), at the very pages cited, the Supreme Court said a court may refuse a lawyer "not only in those rare cases where an actual conflict may be demonstrated before trial, but in the more common cases where a potential for conflict exists," and that a party's choice of lawyer can be overridden "by a showing of a serious potential for conflict." *In re Dresser Industries* (5th Cir. 1992) is a case in which the Fifth Circuit ordered a law firm disqualified. The phrases in quotation marks, "mere possibility of conflict" and "speculative or potential conflicts," appear in neither case.

**Why it matters:** Whether a potential conflict can be enough was a central legal question on Mr. Bass's motion. The response told the court that the cases answer no. The Supreme Court case it cited says that a serious potential conflict can be enough.

United States (U.S. 1988)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112074/) · [In re Dresser Industries (5th Cir. 1992)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/588818/)

### 3. Put three phrases in quotation marks that the cited cases do not contain

**In:** The State's response opposing the motion to disqualify its lawyer (Doc. 50), filed Apr. 28, 2026, pages 4–6 and 12.

**What the filing says:**

> disqualification is “a severe sanction” that must be used sparingly and only when necessary to preserve the integrity of the judicial process. American Airlines, 972 F.2d at 611; Horaist v. Doctor’s Hosp. of Opelousas, 255 F.3d 261, 266 (5th Cir. 2001).

> the movant must demonstrate that the alleged conflict “clearly” threatens the fair and efficient administration of justice. Id.; Horaist, 255 F.3d at 266

**What the record shows:** In the same response, three phrases are in quotation marks, credited to cases that do not contain them. "A severe sanction" (page 4) is cited to *In re American Airlines* (5th Cir. 1992) and *Horaist* (5th Cir. 2001); neither opinion uses the word "severe" or the word "sanction." "Mere possibility of conflict" (page 4) and "speculative or potential conflicts" (page 12) are cited to *Dresser* and *Wheat* (see the previous entry) and appear in neither. A fourth quoted word, "clearly" (page 6), is presented as the standard set by *American Airlines* and *Horaist*. *Horaist* does not contain it. In *American Airlines* it appears in a quotation of a comment to the Texas ethics rules, inside the court's description of one party's argument; the court did not adopt it as its own standard.

**Why it matters:** Quotation marks tell a judge that a court used those exact words. For three of these phrases, the courts cited did not use them at all.

Doctor's Hospital (5th Cir. 2001)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/24456/) · [Texas Disciplinary Rule 1.06, comment 17](https://www.law.uh.edu/libraries/ethics/TRPC/1.06.html)

## Got the court record wrong

These entries are about the documents in the case itself: Mr. Bass's complaint, the school's letters and the filings' own dates.

### 4. Said a November 9 letter raised a charge that first appeared on November 16

**In:** The State's Motion to Dismiss (Doc. 27), filed Jan. 21, 2026, page 3, footnote 2.

**What the filing says:**

> The November 9, 2023 Hearing Notice informed Plaintiff of a fourth allegation that pertained to a November 3, 2023 unprofessional post by Plaintiff on social media.

**What the record shows:** The November 9 letter (the State's Exhibit 1) is titled "Notice of Consolidation of Outstanding Student Matters." It lists 15 pending matters, and none of them is the November 3 post. The post first appears as a charge in the November 16 hearing notice (Exhibit 2), as item 4: "11/3/2023: 'X' post reported to SOM Administration."

**Why it matters:** When Mr. Bass was told of each charge matters to his claim that he did not get fair notice. The footnote puts notice of this charge a week earlier than the documents show.

### 5. Said Mr. Bass's complaint described something it does not describe

**In:** The State's Motion to Dismiss (Doc. 27), filed Jan. 21, 2026, page 16, footnote 5.

**What the filing says:**

> The Complaint references (i) a generalized comment about mortality that did not address any governmental policy, (ii) crude personal communications sent directly to faculty members involving bodily fluids, and (iii) vague criticism of COVID-19 vaccination policies

**What the record shows:** Mr. Bass's First Amended Complaint, the one the motion was answering, says nothing about communications involving bodily fluids. That description matches material in the school's hearing-notice packet (the State's Exhibit 2, page 12), not anything in the complaint. On page 3, the motion itself attributes this charge to the school's hearing notice.

**Why it matters:** When a court tests whether a complaint states a claim, it starts from what the complaint alleges. This footnote presented the school's accusation as something Mr. Bass's own complaint said.

### 8. Certified that filings were served at Mr. Bass's email address, while the attached e-filing records show a misspelled one

**In:** The State's Notice of Submission, filed Mar. 13, 2026, page 2.

**What the filing says:**

> This will certify that a true and correct copy of the foregoing instrument was served in accordance with Rule 21a of the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure upon the counsel of record below on this 13th day of March 2026.

> Email: kbassphiladelphia@gmail.com

**What the record shows:** The e-filing system's own record of service is the next page of the same filing. It lists Mr. Bass at kbassphiladelphi@gmail.com, which is missing the last letter, and reports ERROR. The same thing happened on three more State filings: the Amended Notice of Submission, filed 58 minutes later (ERROR); the State's March 18 reply in support of its plea (sent to the misspelled address); and its April 14 response to Mr. Bass's new-trial motion (ERROR). Of the notices, the reply and a proposed order, the State's appeal brief later said: "The record establishes that Bass did not receive those documents because they were electronically transmitted to an incorrect email address."

**Why it matters:** The two notices said when the State's plea would be submitted to the court for consideration without an oral hearing. The first one gave a date, March 9, 2026, that was four days before the notice itself was filed; the amended notice changed it to March 19. Neither notice reached Mr. Bass, and neither did the State's reply.

### 9. Cited a page of a Texas Supreme Court decision for a rule that is not on that page

**In:** The State's Plea to the Jurisdiction, filed Feb. 9, 2026, page 7.

**What the filing says:**

> Ripeness further requires that an agency or official reach a final, definitive position before judicial intervention is appropriate. Texas Dep’t of Protective & Regulatory Servs. v. Mega Child Care, Inc., 145 S.W.3d 170, 198 (Tex. 2004).

**What the record shows:** Page 198 of *Mega Child Care* discusses whether a state law lets courts review agency decisions, and whether it waives the state's immunity from suit. It says nothing about a "final, definitive position." That phrase appears nowhere in the opinion.

## How this was checked

- **Every entry was checked against the documents themselves:** the State's filing, Mr. Bass's complaint as it stood on the date the State wrote, the exhibits, and the full text of every court decision cited.
- **Every entry rests on exact quotations** from court documents and decisions, each tied to the page it appears on.
- **Arguments are not listed.** This page lists only statements that can be checked against a document.
- **The links go to copies of the court filings on [case.kevinnbass.com](https://case.kevinnbass.com).** They keep the court's page numbering. Personal details are blacked out, and a few pages containing other people's complaints are withheld. Court decisions link to [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com).

## Words used on this page

- **Complaint / petition:** the document that starts a lawsuit and lists what the plaintiff says happened. ("Petition" in Texas state court.)
- **Motion to dismiss / plea to the jurisdiction:** a request to end a case at an early stage. When the question is whether the complaint states a claim, courts generally take its factual allegations as true. Some challenges to a court's jurisdiction can involve evidence.
- **Exhibit:** a document attached to a filing.
- **Doc. 27, Doc. 50, …:** the number of a filing on the federal court's docket.
